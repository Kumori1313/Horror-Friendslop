# Co-op Horror Game Development Roadmap

The first milestone is two friends launching from Game Hub, meeting in a room, and passing a flashlight. Keep that room small and fixed so a misplaced player or prop is easy to diagnose. Once the same test works over a real connection, introduce randomized floor plans and build a complete horror round around them.

Use TypeScript, Three.js, and Vite as the working stack. Start with two desktop browsers. Treat two to four players as a proposed launch target to confirm in playtests. This repository owns the game client. The sibling `game-hub` checkout owns guest identity, room admission, shared migrations, and cross-game tests. Follow the [Game Hub integration guide](game-development-guide.md).

## Phase 1 — Synchronize two players and one object

- Build one lit room with a doorway, first-person movement, collision, and a flashlight either player can pick up, carry, switch on, and drop. Document the install, run, build, and test commands.
- Start with a local TypeScript process and a temporary development room. Let clients send inputs to that process. Have it resolve competing pickups and publish the confirmed owner and position. Smooth remote movement between updates.
- Give the prototype a diagnostic view showing round ID, player positions, flashlight owner, latest update number, and round-trip time. Keep this room as a repeatable test scene as the game grows.

**Pass when** two independent browser sessions see each other walk through the doorway and pass the light. If both grab it at once, exactly one succeeds and both see the beam follow the winner.

## Phase 2 — Prove synchronization under poor connections

- Test on separate devices and across two internet connections. Repeat the handoff with added latency, a brief outage, a backgrounded tab, and a refresh while holding the light. Recover from a fresh snapshot and ignore stale updates from a previous round.
- Record responsiveness, how long the two views disagree, bandwidth per player, and recovery time. Exercise a lost pickup reply and ensure retrying cannot duplicate the item.
- Use those measurements to select the live transport and decide where the simulation runs. Document who validates shared actions, what happens if the host or runtime disappears, and how hosting and traffic fit the hub's $0/month budget. A local server proves the mechanics only.

**Pass when** two remote players finish the handoff under simulated delay, a reconnect restores the correct owner, and losing the runtime produces the documented recovery or clean round ending. Record a repeatable test and a viable deployment choice before integrating.

## Phase 3 — Launch the prototype from Game Hub

- Register the game in the hub catalog and room dispatcher, add its migration and adapter, and connect the game client to the existing Supabase guest and lobby flows. Verify membership before admitting a player to the live round.
- Build both sites and serve them on one local origin. Add hub integration tests for private-code entry, direct room links, refresh, rejected joins, leaving, and starting a fresh round.

**Pass when** two guests enter through the hub, join the same lobby, start the flashlight prototype, and return to the hub with their seats released. A guest outside the room cannot join its simulation.

**Passed on November 20, 2026.** The project owner confirmed the local Game Hub playtest worked. The automated browser test also verified private-code entry, flashlight ownership after refresh, rejection of an outside guest, and seat release before starting a fresh room.

## Phase 4 — Generate shared random layouts

- Author a small kit of rooms and corridors with matching doorways. Generate a connected route from entrance to objective to exit, then place branches and loops so players can split up and regroup. Vary the routes players take as well as the props they see.
- Give each run a seed and generator version. Have the simulation generate one layout and send the room placements, rotations, connections, and stable object IDs to every client. On reconnect, restore that layout alongside current door and item states.
- Check for overlapping rooms, blocked doorways, and unreachable objectives, accounting for player collision size. Bound generation attempts and supply a known playable layout if they fail. Save failing seeds for regression tests.

**Pass when** a repeatable batch of 1,000 seeds yields playable layouts or the documented fallback, selected seeds produce visibly different routes, and two clients agree on walls, doors, and objectives after a refresh. Track fallback frequency so a broken generator cannot quietly ship one map.

**Automated verification passed on November 20, 2026.** Version 1 produced 977 distinct route graphs across seeds 0–999, with zero fallbacks. Two Game Hub guests received identical floor plans. Refreshing the operator who opened the intake bulkhead restored its open state, the same layout, and the held flashlight.

**Two-device playtest passed on November 20, 2026.** Both players joined the same private match through Game Hub and saw the generated facility on their devices.

## Phase 5 — Finish one playable horror round

- Pick one original team objective. For example, one player could trace a fault through a dark building while another operates the breaker panel. Give the team a reason to separate and a reason to find each other again.
- Add one creature with a readable cue and a consistent response, such as investigating the sound of a tripped breaker. Synchronize its decisions, objective progress, player incapacitation, and the round result. Define how teammates recover a player and what ends a failed run.

**Pass when** a fresh group can learn the objective, encounter the threat, win or lose, and start another run on a new layout. Watch where friends coordinate, panic, or laugh before committing to more content.

## Post-launch playtest roadmap

Phases 8–19 build on feedback from the public two-player release. Use the existing creator-hosted private lobby for each playtest. Repeat changes that affect movement, sound, or shared state with a desktop guest and a physical Android host.

Each phase adds geometry, entities, or traffic. Hold a performance budget throughout: record the developer build's frame p95, scene-ready time, and RX/TX on the physical Android host in that phase's heaviest scene, and compare them with the previous release. Keep simulation timers in real seconds even when frames are slow; the creator host catches up in fixed 1/30 s steps. Fix a regression in the phase that caused it.

Playtest feedback after Phase 10 sets the direction from Phase 11 on. Most games in the genre give large creatures open ground, and Relay Room's corridors and small rooms cannot hold them. Like Lethal Company, keep the facility cramped and suited to small creatures, and add an open outdoor area where large ones roam. To make the loop our own, the crew lives in a separate **home dimension**. A rift drops them onto an outdoor map that closes behind them. **Ringing the bell** in front of the facility summons a rift somewhere else outdoors, and enrages every creature. The crew then has to **throw** its salvage into the rift and jump in after it. Maps vary in terrain and weather, and facilities vary in type, starting with the current abandoned factory and a new **Spire**.

## Phase 10 — Replace the relay assignment with a salvage quota

- Prototype a crew contract spanning several expeditions. Both players can explore, carry salvage back to extraction, and choose between another haul and leaving. Sell recovered items for money. Show the target, credited earnings, and expeditions remaining on a terminal at base. Meeting quota wins the contract. Missing it by the deadline loses it.
- Retire the requirement for one operator to wait at Intake or the Switchboard. Let players learn the loop by picking up an object, loading it, and seeing its sale value on the terminal. Make enough loot reachable for the quota to be achievable across the contract.
- Render salvage with shared, instanced meshes, hidden with its room by the doorway culling. Send loot as changes to ownership and position rather than the full item list in every snapshot.
- Let a surviving player deliberately extract while a partner is down. Prototype a **20% deduction from that expedition’s recovered value** for abandoning the partner. For example, 100 in recovered salvage credits 80 if one player leaves their downed partner, or 100 if they rescue and extract them. Show the deduction on the receipt. A brief disconnect or a tap on **Leave to hub** must never count as paid extraction.
- **Compound the deduction for larger lobbies.** Each operator who doesn't make it out applies another 20% reduction to what remains: the crew keeps x × 0.80^d of x recovered with d operators lost. One loss keeps 80, two keep 64, and five still keep about 33 of 100. Adding 20% per operator would reach nothing at five losses.

- **Ready for playtest (generator v4).** A contract runs three expeditions. Each is a fresh facility, generated from the contract seed so that the quota (45% of all three facilities' salvage) can always be met. Intake is base: the crew loads salvage onto a hazard-striped cargo lift, and a terminal shows the quota, the expedition number, and each loaded item's value. The Relay, Switchboard and Exit bay roles are retired. Each operator carries a pack of three items, collected with **C** and dropped with **X**; the flashlight is separate. The first pickup wakes the Bell Diver in the room farthest away. Extraction sells the lift and the extracting operators' packs. Extracting alone past a downed partner deducts 20% and loses their pack. A crew wipe, or a missed rescue, ends the contract. Salvage is derived from each layout's seed, so snapshots carry only the items that have moved, and it is drawn as one instanced mesh per kind, culled with its room. Tests cover quota success, a missed deadline, a crew wipe, rescue, extracting alone with the deduction, solo extraction, a disconnect or leave that never pays out, and a refresh during settlement crediting once. The Game Hub acceptance test plays a two-player haul, refreshes the host on the receipt, moves both operators to the next facility, and returns to the lobbies. Remaining: a two-browser haul, and a fresh crew finding the sale without coaching.

## Phase 11 — Give surfaces real materials

- Stop stretching textures. Give every surface world-space texture coordinates, so a pattern keeps the same size on a 1 m wall stub and a 12 m hall wall, on floors and ceilings, and across ramps and stairs. Mipmap textures, and use anisotropic filtering where the device supports it, so floors stay crisp at grazing angles.
- Build a small material library: painted concrete and plaster, sheet and corrugated steel, diamond-plate floors, open grating for catwalks and stairs, rusted and oil-stained metal, and tile. Give walls, floors, and ceilings different materials. Let each room template choose a palette, so a Machinery hall, a Cold store, and a corridor read differently at a glance. Generate textures in code, or ship CC0 textures with source and license notes.
- Add reflective, metallic surfaces here and there: steel trims, pipes, machine housings, railings, the Intake bulkhead, and salvage. Use physically based materials lit by a small pre-filtered environment map built once per facility, so metal catches the flashlight without real-time reflections.
- Stay inside the frame budget. Keep each room to a few merged draws, share textures across rooms, cap texture memory for phones, and compare frame p95 on the Android host before and after.

- **Ready for playtest.** Texture coordinates come from world position in metres, so every wall, floor, ceiling, ramp, and stair keeps one scale. A library of ten textures is generated in code: concrete, plaster, steel panel, corrugated steel, diamond plate, grating, rust, tile, brushed steel, and wood. Each room kind has its own wall, floor, and ceiling palette. Props keep their colours over painted steel, brushed steel, rust, or wood. Steel walls and floors catch the flashlight as highlights. Brushed-steel props and metal salvage also reflect a dim reflection map, generated once from a small scene. Wall trims stay plain steel, because they line every wall. Anisotropic filtering is capped at 2×. In headless software rendering, which exaggerates per-pixel cost, seed 19 averaged 20.5 ms against Phase 10's 16.7 ms at the spawn, and 24.1 against 18.8 ms facing the terminal, with 11 draws against 9. Measure on the Android host. If it regresses, drop the filtering to 1× or remove the reflection map.
  - **Clipping fixed.** The first playtest saw neighbouring rooms' textures flickering through each other on shared walls and door lintels, and on Intake's amber bands. Adjacent rooms used to draw identical wall boxes in the same place, which had been invisible while every room used one texture. Each room now draws its wall only on its own side of the boundary, 6 cm thick, so collision and every wall face stay where they were. Trims thicken only across the wall, and the Intake bands are drawn once, 5 mm inside the doorway edge. A test checks that no two rooms' walls share any volume. A second pass fixed the steel strip along the foot of each wall, whose end lay flush with the wall's end at doorway edges. It now runs 1.25 cm past each end, on its own room's side, and appears only on walls that stand on a floor. Walls that stop level with an upper floor stop 1 cm short inside the slab. A test now looks for faces of two different materials drawn flush in the same plane anywhere a player can stand, across eight seeds and the stairwell walkthrough.
- **Playtested on November 23, 2026.** In solo play, textures kept one scale and didn't stretch, and the materials were well received. The clipping above was the only problem found. On the Pixel 8a in Vanadium, Frame p95 held at 16.9 ms, the same as Phase 10, so the materials cost nothing measurable on the phone. Loot didn't feel too scarce with ordinary rooms empty 65% of the time. Because the quota is 45% of the salvage the contract actually spawns, scarce luck lowers it too: across 1,000 contracts, the sparsest held 18 items and still left 12 spare beyond the most valuable picks that meet quota. One facility can still hold as few as 3 items.
- **Released on November 23, 2026.** The Pages repository pins build `db0440b`, and `/relay-room/release.json` reports generator version 4. The local release suite passed with Horror-Friendslop `db0440b`, game-hub `bf25679`, tic-tac-toe `245ebb2`, and poker `dfde121` in Playwright Chromium, skipping only the two Counter-Attack Web tests. The published smoke test then passed both the two-guest contract run and the solo test. To roll back, restore `SOURCE_REVISION` to `999056c`.

## Phase 12 — Carry salvage in a hotbar and throw it

- Replace the text pack with a hotbar. Show each slot with a small 3D preview of its item, rendered once per kind and reused. Cycle slots with the scroll wheel or number keys, and tap a slot on touch. Show the selected item in the operator's hands, and show the same item to the partner. The flashlight takes a hotbar slot like any other item, so carrying it costs a slot. Choose the slot count in the prototype, starting from the Phase 10 pack's three items plus the light.
- Let operators either **drop** the selected item at their feet or **throw** it. A throw follows a physical arc, bounces off walls and floors, slides to rest, and can fall down a stairwell or off a gallery. Hold to throw harder, and give heavy items a shorter throw. Thrown items stop at closed bulkheads and never end up inside a wall or outside the facility.
- Keep throws authoritative and cheap. The host simulates each throw in fixed steps on the same collision geometry. Clients receive the launch (start, velocity, time) and replay the identical arc, so a throw costs one message rather than a stream. Snapshots carry only items in flight and their final resting place. A refresh mid-flight lands the item where the host put it.
- Prove throwing before the rift needs it. Throw salvage across a room, down a stairwell, and into a partner's reach, and pick up items your partner threw.

- **Ready for playtest.** Four hotbar slots replace the text pack. Each slot shows a small 3D preview rendered once per kind at startup. **1–4**, the scroll wheel, or a tap selects a slot. The selected item shows in the operator's hands and in the partner's view. **E** picks up the nearest item, the flashlight included, so carrying the light costs a slot. With another slot selected, the flashlight clips to the chest: still lit, but dimmer and shorter, following the body. **X** drops the selected item; holding **T** or the left mouse button, or **Hold to throw** on touch, charges a throw over 0.9 s. Throws are simulated in fixed steps on the collision geometry. Items bounce, slide off props, fall from galleries and stairwells, and stop at closed bulkheads, and heavy items throw a third shorter. A thrown flashlight stays lit and tumbles. The host computes the whole flight at launch; snapshots carry only the launch and elapsed time while the item flies, and clients replay the identical arc. Tests cover a client's replay matching the host, resting spots on 160 random throws across four facilities (on a floor, overlapping nothing, within an operator's reach), throws off a gallery, a closed and then open bulkhead, a refresh mid-flight, a partner picking up a thrown item, a struck operator losing the light's slot, and old checkpoints rebuilding their slots. A one-off stress run of 2,400 throws found no bad resting spot, one fallback to the thrower's feet, and a worst flight that took 2 ms to compute. The Game Hub acceptance test now picks up with **E** and the touch **Pick up** button, checks both hotbars, and throws an item that both clients land in the same spot before the host picks it up again.

Each expedition now draws its map kind from its seed (`src/map-kinds.ts`), so every client agrees without a message; the draw is between the rolling hills and the marsh until the quarry and snowfield join it, and the generator version is 9. A kind sets its weather: the look outdoors (fog, sky, light, far plane), how far creatures see (`sight`), how far sound carries both ways (`carry`), and whether shallow water is mud. The **marsh** is low and flat, its swells a third of the hills' and without ditches, the river still winding through it. About sixteen clusters of still pools lie clear of the plot, the arrival and the river: overlapping circles of water at one level, 0.3 to 0.65 m deep, so wading them is slow (mud slows to 80% from ankle depth, as wading past the knees slows to 60%); about a third hide a sinkhole over the head, steep-sided, that drowns whoever falls in. Each other cluster has a straight plank boardwalk across it on short posts, and trails get decks over stretches where they wade a pool. Every boardwalk reaches out onto dry land at both ends, its last planks easing down to lie half sunk in the bank's mud; each deck is at most a step (0.3 m) from the next, a boardwalk is only laid where both ends come ashore that way (across a cluster, whichever of its two directions does), runs never overlap at different heights, and ruins, wrecks and boulders are kept off the landings. The banks within about 3 m of a pool are mud: a blotchy brown texture with a wet sheen, fading out at its edge, over a darker pool bed. Reeds grow around the pools' edges. Thick fog leaves under 15 m of sight and a 42 m far plane: stalkers see an operator at under half the range (14 m, 22 m with a lit flashlight), and in the still air the crew is heard, and hears creatures, 15% further. The marsh keeps nearly as much cover (7.2 wrecks a map against the hills' 8.1, at least 5) and builds in the same time (about 210 ms). ## Phase 19 — Animate the crew, equipment, and creatures

- Model the items before animating them. Replace the coloured boxes with real models for every piece of salvage, the flashlight, and the bought gear (spare cells, flares, the stun baton), so each is recognisable on sight in hand, on the floor, in flight and in its hotbar preview. Keep them cheap: low-poly, one instanced mesh per kind as now, with the same collision bodies so throws are unchanged.
- Animate idle, walk, stair travel, carrying, tool use, downed, and revive states. Give doors, pickups, flashlight use, throws, the bell, and the rift opening and closing visible transitions. Start creature animation in its encounter phase, then finish ceiling traversal, attacks, retreats, and hit reactions across the roster.
- Drive animations from confirmed gameplay events so a guest sees what the host resolved. Reconnecting mid-action should restore the current pose. Retain reduced-motion controls and measure frame times on the physical Android host in a dark, multi-floor encounter.
- Share skeletons and animation clips between characters of the same kind. Skip animation updates for culled characters, and update distant ones at a lower rate. Use simpler meshes for characters seen across a large hall.

**Pass when** one player can tell what their partner picked up, whether a tool connected, and when a revive finished by watching the action. Run a full public-site contract with two fresh players, record their seed and device performance, and use their observations to tune the next release.

## Phase 20 — Vote on what follows the crew into the next expedition

After each expedition's salvage sale, hold an intermission at the terminal in the home dimension if the contract continues. This is where the crew chooses the modifiers for later expeditions. Prototype two crew votes per intermission. One determines which threat gets worse. The other grants an advantage to both players. Each choice takes effect on the next expedition and lasts through the rest of that contract. A fresh contract starts with the baseline roster, abilities, and terrain.

- **Threat vote.** Offer a new enemy species or an upgrade to one already active. Keep one individual of each chosen species per facility. For example, the crew might admit a scavenger or give the existing roaming enemy a follow-up lunge and higher damage. Once all species are active, offer distinct upgrades to the existing roster.
- **Crew vote.** Offer benefits that change how the pair approaches the next haul. A salvage premium increases the sale value used for both wallet and quota credit, with any abandonment deduction applied afterward. A double jump lets both players cross a broken catwalk while carrying loot. Show the actual benefit on each ballot and retire a one-time ability from future offers once acquired.
- **Terrain progression.** Map and weather draws from Phase 18 can also escalate across a contract. Introduce a basic jump on keyboard and touch. As expeditions advance, generate larger chambers with broken walkways, floor seams, and gaps that require consecutive jumps. Validate required loot and extraction routes against the crew’s current movement abilities. Keep larger chambers inside the doorway culling and the frame budget, and keep layout generation fast enough not to delay the next expedition. A crew that chose money can still make the crossing, while a crew that chose mobility can take a shorter route or reach a valuable cache.
- **Two-player voting.** Show both ballots and selections to both players. For the prototype, alternate which seat breaks a split vote each intermission and identify that seat before voting. The host checkpoints the offers and votes for the expedition so refreshing or switching networks restores the same decision and each winner applies once.

**Pass when** two crews play several expeditions on matched seeds, one favoring earnings and the other mobility. Both should face riskier crossings and more dangerous encounters in later rounds, and each should be able to point to a moment when its chosen buff helped. Compare falls, downs, salvage delivered, and extractions by round to tune the net difficulty increase. Exercise a split vote, reconnect during the ballot, and start a fresh contract to verify the reset.
